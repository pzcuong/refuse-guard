\begin{abstract}
Safety wrappers are being attached to LLM coding assistants faster than their
side effects are measured. We study LLM-based vulnerability analysis on
PrimeVul-derived functions under three threats: safety-induced blocking,
untrusted code context, and the defence layer itself. The blocking fear
dissolves first under measurement: across three open-weight 2--3B models,
including a content-anchored ``risk advisory'' attack whose concrete advisories
name the analyzed function's own sinks in 100\% of cases (vs.\ 0.2\% of naive
context carriers), not one refusal occurs (RR${}=0.000$ over 1{,}200 paired
records; zero refusal in every earlier cell as well). The same attack, however,
silently biases verdicts \emph{toward} ``vulnerable'': Llama flips 10--11 benign
functions per arm (never the reverse), and Granite's vulnerable recall jumps
$0.100\rightarrow 0.733/0.767$. Stronger still, \emph{the defence is the risk}:
a provenance-based safety wrapper collapses Llama's vulnerable recall from
$1.000$ to $0.367/0.333$ (39/60 paired flips, McNemar
$p\leq 3.8\times10^{-6}$), with audited raw outputs confirming genuine
verdicts, not parse failures; a pre-registered component ablation
localizes the harm --- boundary labelling alone is harmless, and the
system task-intent reassertion carries 28 of the 34 net flips (exact
$p=7.45{\times}10^{-9}$). Semantic isolation reduces instruction-injection
success $0.742\rightarrow 0.484$ ($p{=}0.0078$) without improving accuracy, and
ungated refusal-recovery can manufacture full unsafe compliance. We release a
query-relevant, anti-leakage benchmark and argue that at open-model scale the
actionable risk is verdict corruption --- by untrusted context and by the
defence itself --- not denial of service.
\end{abstract}
